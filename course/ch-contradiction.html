    Chapter 7 Contradiction

    Handout What is Proof by Contradiction and Why Do It?
    

    In a proof by contradiction, we
    🔗
    1. Assume the negation
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Reduce to the absurd
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Conclude the negation could not have been true.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Some of the most consequential theorems in history were proved by contradiction.See https://philosophersguild.com/products/proof-is-in-the-pudding-bowls: 2 of the 4 proofs chosen for the bowls are proofs by contradiction.
    🔗

    Activity 7.1.

    We typically prove the following two theorems by contradiction.
    🔗
    1. There are infinitely many prime numbers.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The number \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Why would you use proof by contradiction for these theorems? To answer this, you might discuss: (a) What is the negation and how might that be helpful for starting a proof? (b) What other proof techniques could you try and how would those start?
    🔗
    🔗

    First Example.

    You have already seen a proof by contradiction! See Theorem 3.16 which is a theorem about disjoint subsets.
    🔗
    The theorem and proof are repeated below. Read them, one sentence at a time, and underline sentences that you think will be in every proof by contradiction.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 7.2.

    If \(A = \{x\in\mathbb{Z} : x\equiv 2 \pmod{4}\}\) and \(B = \{y\in\Z : y\equiv 3 \pmod{6}\}\text{.}\) Then \(A\cap B = \emptyset\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We will use a proof by contradiction. That is, we assume \(A = \{x\in\mathbb{Z} : x\equiv 2 \pmod{4}\}\) and \(B = \{y\in\Z : y\equiv 3 \pmod{6}\}\) and \(A\cap B \neq \emptyset\text{.}\) Since \(A\cap B\neq \emptyset\text{,}\) there exists \(x\in\Z\) such that \(x\in A\cap B\text{.}\) Since \(x\in A\text{,}\) \(x\equiv 2\pmod{4}\) and so \(4\mid (x-2)\text{.}\) This means there exists \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\) such that \(x-2 = 4k\text{,}\) or
    \begin{equation*} x= 4k+2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Also, since \(x\in B\text{,}\) we know \(x\equiv 3\pmod{6}\text{.}\) So \(6 \mid (x-3)\) and thus there exists \(j\in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(x-3=6j\) or
    \begin{equation*} x=6j+3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(x=4k+2\) and \(x=6j+3\text{,}\) by substitution and algebra we get,
    \begin{align*} 4k+2 \amp = 6j+3\\ 4k \amp = 6j+1 \\ 2 (2k)\amp =2(3j)+1 \end{align*}
    By closure of integers under multiplication, \(2k\in\mathbb{Z}\) and \(3j\in\mathbb{Z}\text{.}\) Therefore \(2(2k)\) is an even integer and \(2(3j)+1\) is an odd integer. But this is a contradiction - there is no integer that is both even and odd! This contradicts our assumption that \(x\in A\cap B\text{,}\) and thus we conclude \(A\cap B = \emptyset\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 7.3.

    In a proof by contradiction, you want to work towards something obviously absurd. “Even = Odd” is one example of a contradiction you might get. You also could get other contradictions in the proof above - can you think of a different one?
    🔗
    We could have also phrased the above theorem as “For each integer \(n\text{,}\) if \(n\equiv 2 \pmod{4}\) then \(n\not\equiv 3\pmod{6}\text{.}\)” Why would it make sense to use contradiction for that theorem statement (and not, say, contrapositive)? What would change about the proof above?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 7.13. The Prime Divisor Theorem.

    Let \(a\text{,}\) \(b\) and \(c\) be natural numbers. If \(a \mid (bc)\) and \(a\) is prime, then \(a \mid b\) or \(a \mid c\text{.}\)
