    Vocabulary and Notation.

    Word Symbol Meaning
    Statement A statement is a sentence or mathematical expression
    that is true or false.
    Open Sentence An open sentence is a sentence or mathematical expression
    which does not have a truth value, depends on some unknown,
    becomes a statement when the unknown is specified.
    And \(\wedge\) \(P\wedge Q\) is true when both \(P\) and \(Q\) are true.
    Or \(\vee\) \(P \vee Q\) is true when at least one of \(P\) or \(Q\) is true.
    Not (or Negation) \(\sim\) \(\neg P \) has the opposite truth value of \(P \)
    Conditional Statement
    “If...then”    		 \(\implies\) \(P\implies Q\) means “If \(P\) then \(Q\)” and is only false
    in situations where \(P\) is true and \(Q\) is false.
    Hypothesis In a conditional statement “If \(P\) then \(Q\)”, \(P\) is the hypothesis.
    Conclusion In a conditional statement “If \(P\) then \(Q\)”, \(Q\) is the conclusion.
    Biconditional Statement
    “if and only if”, or “iff”    		 \(\Leftrightarrow\) A biconditional statement is of the form \(P\) if and only if \(Q\text{.}\)
    It means \(P\implies Q\) and \(Q\implies P\text{.}\)
    Converse The converse of \(P\implies Q\) is \(Q\implies P\text{.}\)
    Logically Equivalent \(\Leftrightarrow\) A statement is logically equivalent to another statement if they
    have the same truth value on all possible inputs.
    E.g., \(\sim (P \wedge Q) \Leftrightarrow \sim P \,\vee \sim Q\) by DeMorgan’s Laws
    For all \(\forall\) Also, ``For ever", ``For each"
    There exists \(\exists\) Also ``For some"
    Quantifier Indicates the number or quantity of our objects.
    \(\forall\) is the universal quantifier and
    \(\exists\) is the existential quantifier
    Tautology A statement that is true no matter what, e.g., \(S\vee \sim S\)
    Contradiction A statement that is never true, e.g., \(S\wedge \sim S\)
    Converse The converse of \(P\implies Q\) is \(Q\implies P\text{.}\)
    Contrapositive The contrapositive of \(P\implies Q\) is \(\sim Q \implies \sim P\text{.}\)

    Negations.

    Negating a statement should result in the statement having the opposite truth value. Whenever the original statement is true, the negation should be false, and whenever the original statement is false, the negation should be true. Below are some rules for negating statements.
    🔗
    🔗
    Logical Connector In words Negate! Negation distributed In words
    \(\forall x\in A\) For all \(x\) in \(A\) \(\sim(\forall x\in A)\) \(\exists x\in A\) There exists \(x\) in \(A\text{.}\)
    \(\exists x \in A\) There exists \(x\) in \(A\) \(\sim(\exists x \in A)\) \(\forall x\in A\) For all \(x\) in \(A\)
    \(P\vee Q\) \(P\) or \(Q\) \(\sim (P\vee Q)\) \(\sim P \wedge \sim Q\) Not \(P\) and not \(Q\)
    \(P\wedge Q\) \(P\) and \(Q\) \(\sim(P\wedge Q)\) \(\sim P \vee \sim Q\) Not \(P\) or not \(Q\)
    \(P\implies Q\) If \(P\) then \(Q\) \(\sim(P\implies Q)\) \(P\wedge \sim Q\) \(P\) and not \(Q\text{.}\)
    \(\sim P\) Not \(P\) \(\sim(\sim P)\) \(P\) \(P\)
    🔗

    Handout English is hard!
    

    English is hard!

    As we’ve seen, there are many many ways to write a conditional statement. For example, “if \(P\) then \(Q\)” can be written as “\(P\) implies \(Q\)” or “\(Q\) if \(P\)” or “\(P\) only if \(Q\)” and more. (See page 211 of your textbook.) This is similar for the universal and existential quantifiers. For example, instead of “for all”, we might say “for any”, or “for each”. And instead of “there exists” we could say “there is”. There’s also an idea of “hidden quantifiers”. E.g., sometimes instead of starting “for all” we start with “if” and it has the same meaning.
    🔗
    In general, to negate a statement, it’s a good idea to write the statement using symbols, including any hidden quantifiers. On the other hand, sometimes translating a statement into different words, can be easier to interpret.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.12.

    The statement \((\forall x\in\mathbb{R})(x^2\ge 0)\) can be said as
    • For each real number \(x\text{,}\) \(x^2\ge 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The square of every real number is at least \(0\) >.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The square of a real number is at least \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(x\) is a real number, then \(x^2\ge 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The last statement, is a conditional statement, that “hides” the universal quantifier.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.13.

    The statement \((\exists x\in \mathbb{R})(x^2 = 5)\) can be said as
    🔗
    • There exists a real number \(x\) such that \(x^2=5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(x^2=5\) for some real number \(x\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • There is some real number whose square is \(5\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(x^2=5\) has a real number solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    IF

      🔗
      🔗
    • you can establish that the hypothesis \(P\) is , THEN
      🔗
      🔗
    • it necessarily follows that the conclusion \(Q\) .
      🔗
      🔗

    Use this reasoning in what follows.

    Proving Quantified Statements.

    To prove an existentially quantified statement it suffices to provide an example satisfying the criteria. One can do this in two ways:
    🔗
    🔗
    • Constructive: E.g., to prove “there exists a perfect covering of a chessboard” we did so by drawing one out.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Non-constructive: E.g., when applying the pigeonhole principle, we said “if there are 367 people in a stadium, at least two of them are guaranteed to have the same birthday”. We didn’t say which two or what the birthday was, just that we know at least two such people exist.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    To prove a universally quantified statement you must prove for an arbitrary case. E.g., when we proved that the sum of an even integer and an odd integer is odd, it is not enough to say \(2+3\) is odd. We must show for any arbitrary even integer, and any arbitrary odd integer, the sum is odd. We use variables to do infinitely many things at once here!
