    Handout 2.5 Congruence
    

    Modular Arithmetic.

    Modular arithmetic actually shows up lots of places - including in RSA Encryption (which is how your credit card number gets encrypted in an online transactions) and for “check digits” - a way to make sure a UPC bar code has scanned correctly. We introduce a lot of content in MTH 210 for the sake of learning how to write proofs, but modular arithmetic wins the award for “most likely to appear in your future classes”. It’s worth investing the time to make sense of this topic!
    🔗
    The metaphors from the text are a helpful of way understanding modular arithmetic, but we want to define the sandwich in a way that even when the metaphor fails us, we can still agree on what it means for two integers to be congruent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.26.

    For \(a,r\in\mathbb{Z}\) and \(m\in \mathbb{N}\text{,}\) we say that \(a\) is congruent to \(r\) modulo \(m\text{,}\) and we write \(a\equiv r \pmod{m}\text{,}\) if \(m\mid a-r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Note in Definition 2.14 of your text the author says \(m\) is an integer, but in practice, we really only think about \(m\) being a positive integer.
    🔗

    Activity 2.27. Practice with congruence.

    (a)

    Find two integers \(a\) and \(r\) that are congruent modulo \(5\text{.}\) That is, give examples of \(a\) and \(r\) such that \(a\equiv r \pmod{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find all integers \(x\) such that \(x\equiv -2 \pmod{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    True or false: If \(x\) is an integer, then there exists a unique integer \(y\) with \(0\le y\le 5\) such that \(x\equiv y \pmod{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    True or false: If \(ak \equiv bk \pmod{m}\) then \(a\equiv b\mod{m}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 2.28. Direct Proofs with Modulo.

    Do a pre-proof analysis of the following theorem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.29.

    For all integers \(a,b,c,\) and \(d\text{,}\) and all natural numbers \(m\text{,}\) if \(a\equiv b \pmod{m}\) and \(c\equiv d\pmod{m}\) then \(a\cdot c \equiv b\cdot d\pmod{m}\text{.}\)
