    How do you Know Which Type of Proof?

    So far our main proof techniques are direct proofs, and proof by cases. We have also done proofs about different topics, like pigeonhole proofs, chessboard problems, even/odd integers, divisibility, congruence, and sets. The proof on Review Assessment 1 will not be on pigeonhole principle nor chessboard problems (though these topics will be on the Review Asssement). Here’s some recommendations:
    🔗
    1. My biggest recommendation: any statement you are asked to prove, write what you know at the top of your page and what you want to prove at the bottom of the page. It’s hard to prove something if you don’t know where you are going! It’s also hard to prove something if you aren’t organized! Then do the “no thinking” part of the proof, where you write down defintions, both going forward and taking backwards steps.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Two ways to recognize cases: (a) the statement’s hypothesis is something vague like “for all integers” and it’s not clear where to start, (b) the statement has a an “or” in the hypothesis. Note that an “or” in the conclusion does not necessarily mean you need to do a proof by cases. However, the conclusion hints at which cases might be useful to consider. E.g., if the conclusion is \(n^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}\) or \(3 \mid n^3-n\text{,}\) then you might want to consider cases based on the possible remainders when dividing by 3.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. It might help to write down the general structure of each type of proof. Like if you were doing an even/odd proof, what was the overall idea? What about divisibility? Congruence? Each type of set proof? (Like equality, subset, proper subset, disjoint,etc.)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. If you are concerned you are going in circles, grab a piece of scratch paper (will be provided). Take what you have and try to write down only the things you know for sure in a “linear order”. Write down what you know for sure at the top, and write what you want to prove (and it’s equivalents using definitions) at the bottom.

    Think of \(subseteq\) (subset) vs. \(proper subset\) (proper subset)as \(\leq\) and \(<\) When you have \(\leq\text{,}\) the number on the left be can less than or equal to the number on the right. When you have \(<\text{,}\) the number on the left must be strictly less than (not equal to) the number on the right. THe line underneath \(subseteq\) means the same thing as the line under \(\leq\text{.}\) Every proper subset is a subset. Any subset that is equal to the set itself is a subset, but not a proper subset.For example, \(\{1,2\} \subset \{1,2,3\}\) and \(\{1,2\} \subseteq \{1,2,3,\}\text{,}\) but \(\{1,2,3\} \subseteq \{1,2,3\}\) and \(\{1,2,3\} = \{1,2,3\}\text{.}\)

    Example proof (from review assessment 1 from Winter 2026). You will need to turn the “prove that” statement into a theorem. You should make sure to include all the important mathematical parts. For example, it’s okay to not use complete sentences, but it’s not okay to leave out “\(k\) is an integer” in the definition of even or divides.
    🔗

    Theorem 10.2.

    For all integers \(n\) if \(n\not\equiv 0 \pmod{3}\) then \(n^2 \equiv 1\pmod{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(n\in \mathbb{Z}\) and assume \(not\equiv 0 \pmod{3}\text{.}\) By the division algorithm, this means that \(n\equiv 1 \pmod{3}\) or \(n\equiv 2\pmod{3}\text{,}\) so we consider these two cases.
    🔗
    Case 1: Suppose that \(n\equiv 1 \pmod{3}\text{.}\) By the definition of congruence, \(3 \mid n-1\) and by the definition of divides, there exists an integer \(k\) such that \(3k = n-1\text{.}\) So \(n = 3k+1\text{.}\) By substitution and algebra,
    \begin{align*} n^2 \amp =(3k+1)^2 \\ \amp =9k^2 + 6k + 1\\ \amp = 3(3k^2 + 2k) +1. \end{align*}
    Thus, \(n^2-1 = 3(3k^2+2k)\text{.}\) Since the integers are closed under addition and multiplication, \(3k^2 + 2k\in\mathbb{Z}\) and so by the definition of divides \(3\mid n^2 -1 \text{.}\) By the definition of congruence, \(n^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}\text{.}\) Therefore the theorem is true when \(n\equiv 1 \pmod{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Case 2: Suppose that \(n\equiv 2 \pmod{3}\text{.}\) By the definition of congruence, \(3 \mid n-2\) and by the definition of divides, there exists an integer \(k\) such that \(3k = n-2\text{.}\) So \(n = 3k+2\text{.}\) By substitution and algebra,
    \begin{align*} n^2 \amp = (3k+2)^2\\ \amp = 9k^2 + 12k + 4\\ \amp = 9k^2+12k+3 + 1\\ \amp = 3(3k^2 + 4k+1) +1. \end{align*}
    Thus, \(n^2-1 = 3(3k^2+4k+1)\text{.}\) Since the integers are closed under addition and multiplication, \(3k^2 + 4k+1\in\mathbb{Z}\) and so by the definition of divides \(3\mid n^2 -1 \text{.}\) By the definition of congruence, \(n^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}\text{.}\) Therefore the theorem is true when \(n\equiv 2 \pmod{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since the theorem is true for both \(n\equiv 1\pmod{3}\) and \(n\equiv 2\pmod{3}\text{,}\) the theorem is true in all cases.
